**Are We Maximising the Contribution of Early Career Nurses in Digital Transformation?** _A column for Nursing Times Leader_

**Gemma Stacey & TBC - Lincoln Gombeza**

At a recent meeting about implementing a new electronic patient record system, a newly registered nurse suggested a modification to the proposed workflow. The idea was based on how the current system actually functioned at the point of care, and it would likely prevent a bottleneck the designers hadn't considered. The response? "Once you've been working with these systems longer, you'll understand the bigger picture."

We've both seen this pattern from different vantage points. One of us has watched valuable digital insights go unheard. The other has lived the experience of being that voice, learning when technical observations are welcomed and when they're dismissed. What strikes us both is how routinely healthcare organisations pursue digital transformation whilst ignoring the insights of those who will actually use these systems for the next forty years.

**The Digital Native Question**

Healthcare is experiencing unprecedented digital transformation. Electronic health records, remote monitoring, AI-enabled diagnostics, telehealth platforms, digital-first primary care. These technologies will fundamentally reshape how care is designed, delivered and experienced. Yet the voices leading these transformations often come from technical specialists or senior managers, with frontline nursing input sought late in the process rather than from the outset.

Here's what troubles us: early career nurses are often digital natives with intuitive understanding of how technology can and should work. They're currently experiencing frontline practice in ways that senior colleagues may remember rather than know. They understand both technological possibility and care delivery reality. Yet how often are they genuinely positioned as co-designers of digital systems they'll use for decades?

Someone can contribute to designing a university's digital learning platform, evaluate multiple clinical apps during placement, identify workflow inefficiencies in existing systems, then graduate and find their observations about the new e-rostering system aren't worth hearing.

What changed? Not their technical capability. The system's perception of their legitimacy.

**What Fresh Perspective Offers Digital Innovation**

There's evidence suggesting early career nurses notice aspects of digital systems that habituation has rendered invisible. They haven't yet developed workarounds for poor system design. They haven't learned to accept that "the system just works that way." They're still frustrated by inefficiencies that experienced colleagues have learned to tolerate.

This isn't romantic idealism about youth and technology. It's practical observation about what happens when you haven't yet invested years in adapting to suboptimal digital infrastructure. When someone points out that a digital form requires duplicate data entry and hears "yes, but that's just how the system works," it's worth asking whether we've confused familiarity with inevitability.

One of us recently watched a newly qualified nurse demonstrate how a patient engagement app failed basic accessibility standards. The observation was met with "well, patients haven't complained." But patients often don't complain about digital barriers. They simply disengage, and we interpret their disengagement as lack of interest rather than poor design. The early career nurse spotted this because they were still thinking like recent service users themselves, remembering what good digital experience feels like.

**The Humanising Force in Digital Change**

The International Council of Nurses position statement on digital health transformation emphasises that nurses are uniquely positioned to ensure digital innovations remain grounded in person-centred and compassionate values. We are the professional group with the most sustained contact with patients and families across all care settings. We understand care pathways and system complexities. We possess clinical expertise combined with relational skills.

Yet digital transformation initiatives are often led by technical or managerial agendas with nursing input sought after key decisions have been made. This risks creating efficient systems that fail to deliver compassionate care. Technology alone cannot deliver person-centred care. Digital tools must be shaped by those who understand the nuanced, contextual nature of care relationships and who can ensure that efficiency gains don't come at the cost of dignity or therapeutic presence.

Early career nurses can be particularly effective as the humanising force within digital transformation. They maintain current proximity to what it feels like to be on the receiving end of healthcare systems. They remember being students navigating patient portals and health apps. They understand both the digital literacy of their generation and the potential barriers for those less comfortable with technology.

When digital transformation is discussed in abstract terms of "improved workflows" and "enhanced efficiency," early career nurses can ask the grounding questions: but what does this mean for the confused older patient? How does this work when someone's first language isn't English? What happens when the wifi fails? These aren't obstacles to transformation. They're essential design considerations that prevent implementing systems that work beautifully in boardrooms but fail at the point of care.

**The Community and Digital Divide**

The NHS Long Term Plan and similar policy frameworks across the UK emphasise care closer to home, yet the digital infrastructure to support this shift remains underdeveloped. Digital-first primary care, remote monitoring, telehealth and community-based specialist services all require fundamentally different approaches to digital design than hospital-centric systems.

Early career nurses increasingly work across settings. They understand that the digital systems serving acute care don't automatically translate to community contexts. They see firsthand how remote monitoring works brilliantly for some patients and creates anxiety for others. They notice when video consultations genuinely improve access and when they become barriers to care for those without reliable technology or digital confidence.

Where is the voice of early career community nurses in shaping digital transformation? They're the ones actually implementing these systems, troubleshooting when technology fails, explaining to patients how to use digital tools, and witnessing both the possibilities and the failures. Their insights aren't supplementary to digital transformation. They're essential.

**Creating Conditions for Digital Co-Design**

Individual technical capability matters, but systemic conditions matter more. Here's what successful digital co-design with early career nurses requires:

**Protected time for meaningful participation.** Early career nurses can't contribute to digital transformation in the margins of impossible workloads. Genuine participation in system design, testing and evaluation requires protected time. Without it, we're asking people to improve systems they barely have time to use.

**Psychological safety to challenge technical decisions.** Early career nurses need reasonable assurance they can question proposed digital solutions without negative consequences. When someone points out that a new system creates more work than it saves, that observation needs to be welcomed rather than dismissed. Creating space where "this doesn't work" can be said honestly is essential to avoiding expensive digital failures.

**Explicit development in digital health leadership.** Understanding clinical practice doesn't automatically confer capability in health informatics, digital project management or technology evaluation. These skills need explicit development. Assuming early career nurses will naturally acquire digital leadership capabilities without support disadvantages them and weakens digital transformation.

**Demonstrable impact on digital decisions.** When early career nurses participate in digital consultations, pilots or user testing, they need to see how their input influenced outcomes. When feedback disappears with no explanation of whether it shaped final decisions, engagement becomes tokenistic performance. Closing the loop sustains meaningful participation and signals that input genuinely matters.

**Digital Transformation at Human Pace**

Professional socialisation creates tension in digital transformation contexts. Organisations need nurses who can work within existing digital systems. Simultaneously, healthcare needs people who question whether those systems serve patients and staff well. These goals can conflict.

Watch what happens over someone's first year with a new digital system. They arrive noticing inefficiencies, workarounds and barriers to person-centred care. They attempt to raise concerns. If met with "that's just how the system works" or "you'll understand when you've been using it longer," they learn it's safer to stay quiet. Within months, they've stopped reporting issues. They've developed the same workarounds everyone else uses. They've been successfully socialised into accepting suboptimal digital infrastructure.

Healthcare desperately needs people who question digital systems that don't work well, who spot when technology creates barriers rather than enables care, who notice when digital efficiency comes at the cost of therapeutic relationships. We're creating environments where these qualities get systematically diminished. Then we wonder why digital transformation initiatives fail to deliver expected benefits.

**What Partnership Looks Like in Practice**

We're not suggesting early career nurses should lead digital strategy or that technical expertise doesn't matter. Understanding health informatics, cybersecurity, interoperability and data governance requires specialist knowledge. Strategic vision for digital transformation deepens with exposure to multiple contexts and technologies.

But different forms of expertise serve different purposes. Technical specialists understand what's possible with current technology. Early career nurses understand what's needed at the point of care. Both are essential, working in complement.

What would genuine partnership look like in digital transformation? It might mean:

**Early career nurses on digital steering groups.** Not as token representatives, but as full participants in shaping digital strategy. With protected time, explicit development support and genuine influence over decisions affecting systems they'll use for decades.

**Co-design from project inception.** Involving early career nurses when digital projects are being scoped, not after solutions have been selected. Their insights about current workflow realities, patient needs and staff capabilities inform better design decisions than consultations after implementation failures.

**User testing that actually influences design.** Moving beyond "tell us what you think" sessions that change nothing, to genuine iterative development where frontline feedback shapes system evolution. Early career nurses should see their observations reflected in modified designs.

**Recognition of digital leadership contributions.** Acknowledging that participating in digital transformation isn't additional duties on top of clinical work, but legitimate professional contribution. Supporting early career nurses who develop expertise in digital health as valid career progression.

**The Digital Transformation We Need**

Digital transformation in healthcare is inevitable and accelerating. The question isn't whether change will happen, but how and in whose interests. By positioning early career nurses as genuine partners in digital innovation rather than end users of solutions designed by others, we can ensure transformation serves the fundamental purpose of healthcare: to alleviate suffering and promote human flourishing.

This requires courage to claim professional authority in spaces traditionally dominated by technical or managerial perspectives. It requires creating cultures where challenge to digital decisions is welcomed rather than discouraged. It requires investing in the conditions that enable early career nurses to contribute their distinctive insights about technology, care and what actually works at the point of practice.

We're facing workforce pressures, increasing complexity and technological change that will fundamentally reshape healthcare delivery. The digital systems we implement now will define nursing practice for decades. Can we really afford to design them without meaningful partnership with those who will use them longest?

**An Invitation to Experiment**

This column is itself an experiment in the partnership we're advocating for. Two people at different career stages, bringing complementary perspectives to digital transformation. It's not the only way forward, but it's one worth trying.

The next time an early career nurse questions a digital system's design, points out workflow inefficiencies or suggests alternative approaches, how will we respond? Will we explain why they don't understand yet, or will we genuinely consider whether they're noticing something important?

The answer reveals whether we're ready for digital transformation that genuinely serves patients and staff, or whether we're simply digitising existing problems whilst missing opportunities to do things better.

Healthcare digital transformation needs the pattern recognition that experience provides and the pattern interruption that fresh perspective offers. Both matter. The question is whether we're ready to create the conditions where both can genuinely inform the systems we're building.
