**Research Plan: Developing the Person-Centred Digital Healthcare Evaluation Framework (PC-DHEF) for Smartphone-Based Technologies**

**Background and Rationale**

**The Problem**

Digital health technologies, especially smartphone applications that extend or substitute traditional clinician-patient interactions, are now widespread across NHS services. Around 10% of UK adults now use AI chatbots for symptom assessment (Ayre et al., 2025), and many patients increasingly seek health information and support through smartphone apps than traditional clinical consultations (Lundeen et al., 2025). This trend, accelerated by the COVID-19 pandemic, shows no sign of reversing (Senbekov et al., 2020).

Despite this rapid growth, evaluation approaches remain narrowly focused on technical performance, clinical effectiveness, and cost‑efficiency (Gentili et al., 2022; NICE, 2024). Critical dimensions of person-centred care, such as care relationships, patient empowerment, equitable access, and effects on vulnerable populations, are inconsistently assessed (Pfitzer et al., 2024). Our pre-registered scoping review (Bond & Stacey, 2025) confirms that most success measures privilege productivity and efficiency over person‑centred outcomes.

These omissions create substantial risks. Digital health tools that appear clinically effective may, in practice, undermine person‑centred care through poor integration with clinical workflows (Darzi, 2024), exacerbate health inequities through culturally unresponsive design (Farre et al., 2023), or impose disproportionate burdens on those already disadvantaged (Herd & Moynihan, 2018). Recent evidence that some patients view AI chatbots as more empathetic than clinicians (Chen et al., 2025; Ovsyannikova et al., 2025) highlights how profoundly these technologies can reshape care relationships, yet such relational effects remain absent from current evaluation frameworks.

This gap compounds the reality of digital poverty, defined by the Digital Poverty Alliance (2022) as the inability to engage fully with the online world when, where, and how an individual needs to. Digital poverty extends beyond the idea of a digital divide; it reflects intersecting barriers, devices and connectivity, affordability and data costs, digital capability, motivation, and support networks. Research shows that digital poverty both arises from and reinforces socioeconomic inequality, creating a vicious cycle in which financial hardship limits online participation, which in turn constrains opportunities for health access, economic improvement, and social inclusion (Helsper, 2021; Digital Poverty Alliance, 2022).

In the context of smartphone‑based health technologies, digital poverty manifests through inadequate or outdated devices, limited connectivity, and data poverty, issues particularly acute among lower‑income groups (Ofcom, 2022; Digital Poverty Alliance, 2022; Helsper, 2017). Evaluation frameworks must therefore capture not just whether technologies 'work' in optimal conditions, but how digital poverty creates differential experiences and outcomes across populations.

The rapid expansion of smartphone-based digital health technologies has significant workforce implications, particularly for nurses, who form the largest professional group within NHS services and are central to the delivery of digitally mediated care. Evidence demonstrates that implementation success depends not only on technical performance or clinical efficacy, but on workforce acceptance, integration into everyday practice, and sustained use; without this, technologies that appear effective may be underutilised or generate unintended harms (Wynn et al., 2023). Applying the Unified Theory of the Use and Acceptance of Technology (UTAUT) to nursing, Wynn et al. (2023) demonstrated that age, gender, experience, and perceived voluntariness of use shape nurses' confidence, perceptions of usability, and willingness to engage with digital tools. These findings illustrate that engagement with smartphone-based technologies is contingent on workforce characteristics and contextual conditions, rather than being an inherent property of the technology itself. However, while models such as UTAUT help explain patterns of adoption and use, these determinants are rarely captured within existing evaluation frameworks. Smartphone-based technologies may also reconfigure clinical work by redistributing cognitive, relational, and documentation labour, particularly where clinicians absorb additional work to mitigate digital poverty or system limitations. Evaluation frameworks that overlook these workforce-mediated effects risk misjudging feasibility, sustainability, and equity.

The PC-DHEF responds directly to this gap by embedding workforce-sensitive and person-centred criteria into the evaluation of digital technologies, ensuring that assessment extends beyond effectiveness to consider impacts on clinical work, professional agency, and the enactment of person-centred care as experienced by the professionals who recommend, use, or resist these technologies in real-world settings.

Whilst guidance from the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) on technology appraisal (2025) incorporates consideration of patient and professional experience, as well as qualitative impacts of new technologies, patient-centredness is not articulated as an explicit evaluative construct. Moreover, NICE acknowledges that the synthesis of evidence is frequently undermined by heterogeneity in evaluation methods. This underscores the value of a standardised approach for assessing how smartphone-based health technologies support person-centred care, enabling more robust and meaningful comparisons between products to inform practice.

**Why This Research Is Needed Now**

The NHS commitment to digital transformation makes robust evaluation frameworks urgently necessary. Lord Darzi's 2024 review warned that digitalisation efforts have often been compromised by poor integration and unintended consequences for both frontline staff and patients. Public opinion reflects similar unease: research by the Health Foundation shows widespread concern about the impact of digital health technologies on person‑centred care, with 71% of people preferring serious diagnoses to be delivered in person rather than through digital means (Horton & Hardie, 2021).

Smartphone‑based technologies that bridge or substitute clinician-patient interactions present distinctive evaluation challenges that are intensified by digital poverty. Systems that support existing clinical encounters can fundamentally alter or replace traditional care relationships; doing so within a social context where millions continue to experience digital deprivation. The Digital Poverty Alliance (2022) identifies five intersecting determinants (devices and connectivity, affordability, capability, motivation, and support) that shape who can access and benefit from digital health technologies. These determinants interact cumulatively: lower‑income households often face higher mobile data costs while owning less capable devices, creating what researchers term "relative digital deprivation," in which the gap between the digitally affluent and digitally poor widens even as overall connectivity improves (Helsper, 2017, 2021; Digital Poverty Alliance, 2022).

For populations facing socioeconomic disadvantage, these barriers compound existing health vulnerabilities (Jackson, 2025). Data poverty, the inability to afford sufficient, private, and secure mobile or broadband data, prevents many from fully utilising smartphone health apps that assume unlimited data access (Good Things Foundation, 2021). The Lancet and Financial Times Commission has argued that digital access should be considered a new determinant of health given technology's significant impact across the life course (Kickbusch et al., 2021). Consequently, evaluation frameworks must capture how smartphone technologies interact with digital poverty to either mitigate or exacerbate health inequities, examining not just technological affordances but crucially their accessibility and usability for digitally impoverished populations.

Person‑centredness, defined as 'an approach to practice established through the formation and fostering of healthful relationships between all care providers, service users and others significant to them, underpinned by values of respect for persons, individual right to self‑determination, mutual respect and understanding' (McCormack & McCance, 2017), provides a crucial theoretical lens. Originally developed for traditional care settings, this framework has yet to be systematically applied to digital contexts where care is mediated through technology itself, and where digital poverty limits participation. Evaluation, therefore, must address not only technological affordances such as effectiveness and functionality, but also social affordances, how smartphone apps enable or constrain person‑centred processes when they mediate or replace clinician-patient interactions, particularly under conditions of digital poverty.

**Evidence Gap and Research Justification**

Our pre-registered scoping review (Bond & Stacey, 2025), which systematically maps how 'success' is defined and measured in digital health innovation, demonstrates that no comprehensive evaluation framework exists that systematically integrates person-centred care principles with equity considerations for smartphone-based digital health technologies. This ongoing review, forming the conceptual foundation for the proposed research, confirms that existing frameworks focus predominantly on technical performance, scalability, and economic value (Azevedo et al., 2021; Gentili et al., 2022), with person-centred outcomes inconsistently assessed (Pfitzer et al., 2024) and equity considerations rarely systematically integrated. The scoping review findings will be synthesised with stakeholder interview data and used to develop the initial programme theory for the realist review (Month 1-3), ensuring the realist synthesis builds on rather than duplicates existing mapping work.

The Person-Centred Practice Framework (McCormack & McCance, 2017, 2021) offers robust theoretical foundations but requires translation to digital health contexts. Existing frameworks like NICE Evidence Standards emphasise technical effectiveness and cost-benefit analysis but provide limited guidance on person-centred or equity dimensions (NICE, 2024). Research examining digital health impacts on vulnerable populations demonstrates complex patterns of benefit and harm requiring nuanced evaluation approaches (Farre et al., 2023; Heaslip et al., 2025).

The concept of digital poverty helps articulate why person-centred evaluation of smartphone technologies requires moving beyond binary measures of 'access' to examine meaningful connectivity and participation. The Alliance for Affordable Internet's 'meaningful connectivity' framework emphasises that adequate digital participation requires everyday access to appropriate devices, sufficient data, and fast enough connections, standards that many economically disadvantaged populations cannot meet (Alliance for Affordable Internet, 2020). Good Things Foundation's Data Poverty Lab proposes that digital poverty solutions must be cheap, handy, enough, safe, and suitable (Good Things Foundation, 2021) - principles directly applicable to evaluating whether smartphone health technologies genuinely serve disadvantaged populations or merely shift service burdens onto those least equipped to manage them.

Digital poverty research also highlights how evaluation must account for 'narrow users' who engage with digital services in limited ways due to device or connectivity constraints, experiencing greater risks of harm and reduced benefits (Digital Poverty Alliance, 2022). For smartphone health technologies, this means evaluation frameworks must capture whether technologies accommodate variable digital capabilities and resources, or whether they implicitly assume digital affluence, thereby excluding or disadvantaging populations most in need of accessible healthcare.

Critically, this research adopts a realist approach recognising that smartphone technologies do not simply 'work' or 'not work' but operate differently depending on context, population characteristics (including digital poverty status), and implementation approaches (Pawson et al., 2005). Understanding what works, for whom, in what circumstances, in what respects, and how is essential for developing evaluation frameworks capturing this complexity.

Before investing in large-scale validation studies, robust conceptual and methodological development is required. This research provides essential groundwork by developing a theoretically-grounded, empirically-informed framework through realist review and meaningful engagement with communities most affected by digital health inequities and digital poverty.

**Aim**

To develop the Person-Centred Digital Healthcare Evaluation Framework (PC-DHEF) for smartphone-based technologies that bridge or substitute clinician-patient interactions, systematically integrating person-centred care principles with equity considerations through realist review and co-production with context expert groups.

**Research Questions**

1\. What mechanisms influence how smartphone-based digital health technologies that bridge or substitute clinician-patient interactions affect person-centred care outcomes, particularly for populations experiencing socioeconomic disadvantage and digital poverty?

2\. For whom do these technologies support or hinder person-centred care, in what contexts, and why?

3\. What essential domains, sub-domains, and indicators should comprise an evaluation framework capturing person-centred and equity impacts of these technologies?

**Objectives**

1\. Conduct a realist review synthesising evidence on mechanisms, contexts, and outcomes of smartphone-based digital health technologies that bridge or substitute clinician-patient interactions, focusing on person-centred care dimensions and differential impacts across populations

2\. Establish and engage context expert groups comprising community members experiencing socioeconomic disadvantage (including digital poverty), clinicians, and technology developers throughout the review process to ensure findings are grounded in lived experience and practical realities

3\. Develop a comprehensive evaluation framework (PC-DHEF) with clearly defined domains, sub-domains, and indicators for assessing person-centred and equity impacts of smartphone-based digital health technologies

4\. Produce actionable recommendations for integrating person-centred evaluation into NHS procurement, implementation, and post-implementation review processes, alongside guidance for developers on designing smartphone technologies that actively support rather than undermine person-centred care relationships

**Design and Methodology**

This research employs realist review methodology following RAMESES (Realist And Meta-narrative Evidence Syntheses: Evolving Standards) guidelines (Wong et al., 2013; Pawson et al., 2005). Realist review asks what works, for whom, in what circumstances, in what respects, and how, making it ideally suited for developing evaluation frameworks addressing complex interventions with heterogeneous effects. Consistent with rapid realist review principles (Saul et al., 2013), our approach is explicitly designed to engage knowledge users throughout and produce actionable findings within an 18-month timeframe.

Our approach integrates systematic evidence synthesis with meaningful engagement of context expert groups throughout, ensuring framework development is theoretically robust, empirically grounded, and practically relevant. Recognising that realist reviews can require considerable time investment (Saul et al., 2013), we have carefully scoped this project to balance comprehensiveness with feasibility, focusing our synthesis on smartphone technologies that bridge or substitute clinician-patient interactions rather than attempting to cover all digital health technologies.

**Context Expert Groups**

Following principles from rapid realist review methodology (Saul et al., 2013), three context expert groups will be established and engaged iteratively throughout the review process to accelerate searching, validate findings, and ensure practical relevance. Each group comprises 8-10 members:

Community Context Expert Group: Community members with lived experience of socioeconomic disadvantage and direct experience of using (or attempting to use, or choosing not to use) smartphone-based health technologies. These are patients and service users themselves, not professional advocates speaking on their behalf. Recruitment through community organisations, food banks, housing associations, and health advocacy groups serving disadvantaged populations. Recognising socioeconomic disadvantage intersects with age, ethnicity, disability, digital literacy, and other marginalised identities. Our recruitment explicitly includes people experiencing digital poverty-those with limited devices (smartphone-only users), data poverty (insufficient or unaffordable mobile data), or intermittent connectivity-as these lived experiences of digital exclusion are essential for developing evaluation frameworks that capture rather than obscure barriers to smartphone health technology use. Digital poverty research demonstrates that exclusion is 'complicated, messy, and intersectional' (Digital Poverty Alliance, 2022), requiring framework development grounded in diverse experiences rather than assumptions about 'typical' users. Members compensated per NIHR INVOLVE guidance (£25/hour plus expenses).

Clinical Context Expert Group: Frontline clinicians across primary care, community nursing, mental health, and acute services with experience of smartphone technologies affecting their practice. Recruited through NHS partnerships and professional networks. Sessions during protected professional development time.

Technology Developer Context Expert Group: Developers, designers, and product managers from healthtech companies creating smartphone applications bridging or substituting clinical interactions. Recruited through Future Nurse networks and healthtech industry connections.

All groups meet three times during the project (months 3, 8, 12) with additional asynchronous engagement between meetings. Sessions facilitated by team members with expertise in co-production and inclusive research practices, with accessible formats, interpretation services, and reasonable adjustments as needed. Following Saul et al.'s (2013) recommendations, context expert groups serve dual functions: accelerating identification of key literature and grey literature sources and validating emerging findings to ensure consistency with practice knowledge and lived experience.

**Phase 1: Realist Review (Months 1-12)**

Following RAMESES standards (Wong et al., 2013), the review progresses through iterative stages:

Step 1: Initial Theory Development (Months 1-3)

Develop initial programme theory explaining how smartphone technologies that bridge or substitute clinician-patient interactions are intended to work, for whom, and in what contexts. Drawing on:

- Person-Centred Practice Framework (McCormack & McCance, 2017, 2021) as primary theoretical foundation, examining how person-centred care domains manifest within digital technological spaces
- Burden of Treatment Theory (May & Montori, 2014) examining how technologies affect patient workload and capacity
- Digital poverty and health equity literature examining structural barriers including the Digital Poverty Alliance's five determinants (devices/connectivity, affordability, capability, motivation, support), concepts of data poverty and meaningful connectivity (Good Things Foundation, 2021; Alliance for Affordable Internet, 2020), and how digital exclusion functions as both cause and consequence of socioeconomic disadvantage (Helsper, 2021; Digital Poverty Alliance, 2022)
- Normalisation Process Theory (NPT) as substantive theory (Dalkin et al., 2021) to understand how smartphone health technologies become embedded (or fail to embed) into routine practice. NPT provides a lens for examining the 'work' involved in implementation (Coherence, Cognitive Participation, Collective Action, Reflexive Monitoring) and will inform development of CMO configurations, particularly mechanisms explaining differential uptake and sustainability across contexts (May & Finch, 2009)
- Initial scoping of literature on smartphone health applications bridging or substituting clinical interactions
- Preliminary understanding of technology's social affordances, how design features enable or constrain person-centred processes when technologies mediate care relationships
- Consultation with Context Expert Group 1 (Month 3) refining theory from experiential perspectives, ensuring it captures social contexts and lived realities of using or being excluded from these technologies in daily life, not just clinical or academic abstractions. Following rapid realist review methodology (Saul et al., 2013), this consultation also identifies key grey literature sources and documents that might otherwise be missed in database searching

Step 2: Systematic Search Strategy (Months 2-5)

Comprehensive search across multiple databases (MEDLINE, CINAHL, PsycINFO, Scopus, Embase) and grey literature sources. Search strategy combines terms for:

- Smartphone technologies (mobile health, mHealth, health apps, digital therapeutics, AI chatbots, symptom checkers)
- Functions bridging/substituting clinical interactions (remote consultation, triage, self-management, symptom assessment, health advice)
- Person-centred outcomes (patient experience, care relationships, empowerment, dignity, shared decision-making)
- Equity dimensions (access, digital exclusion, digital poverty, health inequities, vulnerable populations)
- Evaluation and measurement

Search strategy developed with information specialist, refined iteratively based on emerging findings. Grey literature includes policy documents, technology evaluation reports, and implementation studies. Consistent with rapid realist review methodology (Saul et al., 2013), context expert groups are polled at project initiation to identify documents of key importance, accelerating the search process whilst ensuring comprehensiveness through forward and backward citation searching of key articles.

Step 3: Selection and Appraisal (Months 4-7)

Studies selected based on relevance to programme theory refinement, not traditional inclusion/exclusion criteria. Includes diverse evidence: quantitative studies, qualitative research, mixed-methods evaluations, case studies, implementation reports, and theoretical papers.

Quality appraisal focuses on methodological rigour and conceptual richness rather than applying hierarchical quality criteria. Two reviewers independently assess relevance and rigour using adapted realist appraisal tools (Wong et al., 2013).

Step 4: Data Extraction and Synthesis (Months 5-11)

Extract data on Context-Mechanism-Outcome configurations (CMOs):

- Context: What conditions (patient characteristics, socioeconomic factors, digital poverty status, healthcare settings, technology features, implementation approaches) enable or constrain mechanisms?
- Mechanism: What responses do smartphone technologies trigger (trust, empowerment, burden, care quality perceptions, behaviour changes) amongst different stakeholder groups?
- Outcome: What person-centred and equity outcomes result (care relationship quality, patient empowerment, access patterns, health outcomes, experience)?

NPT constructs will be used to enhance explanatory power of the realist analysis (Dalkin et al., 2021), particularly informing identification of mechanisms within CMO configurations. For example, NPT's Coherence construct can illuminate mechanisms around sense-making about technology purpose; Cognitive Participation can reveal mechanisms around engagement and buy-in; Collective Action can expose mechanisms around the actual work of using technologies; and Reflexive Monitoring can highlight mechanisms around appraisal and adaptation. This integration of NPT within realist logic allows examination of how changes in context trigger specific implementation-related mechanisms to produce person-centred and equity outcomes.

Synthesis employs realist logic of analysis (Pawson, 2006), building explanatory accounts through:

- Juxtaposition: Comparing CMO configurations across studies
- Reconciliation: Explaining discrepant findings through contextual differences
- Adjudication: Assessing evidence strength for different theoretical propositions
- Consolidation: Building cumulative explanatory theory

How findings inform next stages: The synthesised CMO configurations directly inform framework domain development by identifying which person-centred care dimensions are most frequently affected by smartphone technologies, which populations (including those experiencing digital poverty) experience differential impacts, and what contextual factors determine whether technologies support or undermine person-centred care. For example, if synthesis reveals that smartphone triage tools consistently reduce care continuity for older adults with limited digital literacy (CMO configuration), this informs both a 'care continuity' domain in the framework and specific equity indicators examining differential impacts by digital literacy and age.

Context Expert Group 2 (Month 8) reviews these emerging CMO configurations, identifying gaps in academic literature (e.g., missing perspectives from specific marginalised communities or those experiencing digital poverty), challenging interpretations that don't align with lived experience, and providing contextual insights about mechanisms. Their feedback may redirect literature searching or prompt additional grey literature searching to capture missing perspectives. This validation process is essential for ensuring findings resonate with practice knowledge (Saul et al., 2013).

Step 5: Theory Refinement and Framework Development (Months 9-12)

Synthesise refined programme theory into preliminary evaluation framework domains. Map domains to:

- Person-Centred Practice Framework constructs (McCormack & McCance, 2017)
- Institute of Medicine quality dimensions (safe, effective, patient-centred, timely, equitable, efficient)
- Identified mechanisms and contexts affecting person-centred outcomes

How synthesis informs framework: CMO configurations are translated into evaluable domains by identifying: (1) which person-centred care dimensions are most consistently affected by smartphone technologies (becoming core domains), (2) what contextual factors moderate these effects (becoming equity considerations within domains), and (3) what measurable indicators would capture these impacts (becoming specific metrics within domains). For example, if synthesis reveals mechanisms around trust erosion when AI chatbots provide standardised responses failing to acknowledge cultural context, this informs a 'culturally responsive care' sub-domain with indicators examining whether technologies adapt to diverse cultural contexts and communication preferences.

Context Expert Group 3 (Month 12) reviews preliminary framework through structured exercises examining: Does each domain capture something that matters to their lived experience? Are domains comprehensive (covering all important impacts) yet parsimonious (avoiding redundancy)? Are proposed indicators practically assessable? Would framework identify technologies that genuinely support versus undermine person-centred care from their perspective? Their feedback directly shapes which domains are retained, refined, or reconceptualised before Workshop 1.

**Phase 2: Framework Finalisation and Implementation Guidance (Months 13-18)**

Framework Development Workshops (Months 13-15)

Two facilitated workshops bringing together all context expert groups plus additional stakeholders (NHS digital leads, procurement specialists, patient advocates, academic experts):

Workshop 1 (Month 13): Review and refine evaluation domains, sub-domains, and indicators. Ensure framework is comprehensive, parsimonious, and practically implementable. Examine whether domains adequately capture differential impacts across populations, including those experiencing digital poverty.

Workshop 2 (Month 15): Develop implementation guidance addressing:

- How framework applies at different stages (procurement, design, implementation, post-implementation evaluation)
- Data collection methods and measurement approaches for each domain
- Interpretation guidance for assessing person-centred and equity impacts
- Resource requirements and practical considerations

Framework Refinement and Documentation (Months 15-17)

Integrate workshop feedback into final framework structure. Develop comprehensive documentation including:

- Theoretical foundations and evidence base
- Domain definitions with illustrative examples
- Measurement approaches and data sources
- Implementation guidance for different contexts and purposes
- Case examples demonstrating framework application

Knowledge Mobilisation Planning (Month 17-18)

Consistent with rapid realist review emphasis on knowledge use (Saul et al., 2013), final project phase focuses on producing outputs tailored to specific audiences and establishing connections with potential users. Context Expert Groups review draft framework documentation, ensuring accessibility across audiences. Co-develop dissemination strategy targeting:

- NHS procurement and digital transformation teams
- Technology developers and commissioners
- Clinical leaders and frontline staff
- Patient advocacy organisations
- Academic and policy audiences

Final month focuses on producing accessible outputs and establishing connections with potential users to support future validation and implementation.

**Equality, Diversity and Inclusion**

This research explicitly centres equity by focusing framework development on understanding differential impacts of smartphone technologies across populations experiencing disadvantage. Our approach recognises that socioeconomic disadvantage intersects with age, ethnicity, disability, digital literacy, language, and other dimensions of marginalisation, creating compound barriers to healthcare access and digital inclusion (Lopez & Gadsden, 2017).

The Community Context Expert Group will purposively include diversity across these intersecting characteristics, treating complexity as essential rather than challenging. Realist methodology's emphasis on for whom and in what contexts directly addresses how technologies work differently for different populations in different circumstances, moving beyond simplistic 'what works' questions to examine mechanisms of inequity production and mitigation.

Following principles from Patient-Centred Equity Design (May et al., 2025), we examine how digital technologies may perpetuate or challenge structural inequities through:

- Misallocation: Investment concentrated in areas/populations already advantaged, or algorithms trained on non-representative data performing poorly for underrepresented groups
- Mis-implementation: Technologies failing to fit users' lived realities through culturally unresponsive design, language barriers, or approaches not accounting for legitimate mistrust
- Misidentification: Technologies imposing normative expectations excluding those unable to conform through narrow population calibration or biased clinical pathways

Framework development will explicitly address these dimensions, ensuring evaluation approaches can identify rather than obscure equity implications.

Digital poverty research emphasises that exclusion operates through relative as well as absolute deprivation. In highly digitised societies like the UK, having outdated devices, limited data allowances, or slower connectivity creates widening gaps between the digitally affluent and digitally poor even as absolute connectivity rates increase (Helsper, 2017, 2021). The COVID-19 pandemic starkly exposed this divide: whilst national statistics suggested 98% of UK properties could access decent internet, 1.5 million adults remained offline and 1.2-1.7 million children lacked home access to laptops or tablets (Digital Poverty Alliance, 2022). Our framework development must therefore evaluate whether smartphone technologies require levels of digital resource access (device capability, data consumption, connectivity speeds) that create or exacerbate relative deprivation, and whether evaluation approaches can distinguish technologies that work only for digitally advantaged populations from those genuinely accessible across the digital poverty spectrum.

Project management and dissemination will employ accessible formats and multiple engagement channels reducing barriers to participation. All activities will meet accessibility standards with reasonable adjustments provided proactively.
